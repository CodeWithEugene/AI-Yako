## 1. The Meta-Problem. AI Is Rented, Not Owned

The AI revolution runs on a simple arrangement. Your data travels to someone else's data center, you pay per token in US dollars, and the whole thing stops the moment the network does. That arrangement works well in San Francisco. It works badly in Nairobi, and it fails almost entirely for the people who could benefit from AI the most.

Five grounded facts define the problem.

### 1.1 Most people are offline, or one bundle away from it

- Kenya had **27.4 million internet users at the start of 2025, a penetration rate of 48.0%**. That leaves **29.6 million people (52.0%) offline** in a country of 57.0 million (DataReportal, Digital 2025 Kenya).
- Across the continent, **more than 600 million people remain offline** (GSMA, 2026).
- Even the connected pay metered prices. Average pay-as-you-go data in Kenya costs about **KES 4.47 per MB** (Communications Authority, Q4 2024/25). Every cloud AI call spends someone's airtime.
- The direction of travel is mobile. **83.5% smartphone penetration in Kenya** (CA, Q4 2024/25), and smartphones are expected to carry **81% of Africa's mobile connections by 2030**, up from 51% in 2023 (GSMA). The device is already in people's hands. The intelligence just isn't on it.

### 1.2 The compute lives far away and bills in dollars

- Africa holds **less than 1% of global data center capacity while being home to 18% of the world's population** (GSMA, Nov 2025).
- Cloud AI bills arrive in USD while incomes are in shillings and naira. GSMA describes startups facing "variable USD costs" from API fees compounded by volatile exchange rates and high bandwidth costs.
- A typical long-form or image prompt costs **$0.02 to $0.10 per call** before integration costs (Access Partnership, Oct 2025). That is invisible to a Silicon Valley hobbyist and significant to a Kenyan kiosk owner.

### 1.3 Privacy leaves with the packet

- Cloud AI means conversations, documents, health questions, and business data cross borders into someone else's logs. On-device processing "promotes data privacy and protection by bringing data closer to the end user" and removes the need to transmit user data at all (Access Partnership, Oct 2025).
- For clinics, schools, lawyers, and anyone handling other people's information, this is not a preference. It is a duty.

### 1.4 The AI mostly doesn't speak your language

- Africa has **more than 2,000 languages, roughly a third of all languages on Earth** (UNESCO, 2025). **Less than 2% of them have a real online presence** (UNESCO). **Less than 1% of web content exists in African languages** (GSMA, 2026), and **less than 0.1% of global AI training data originates from Africa** (GSMA, 2026).
- **Swahili has over 200 million speakers** (UNESCO, 2025), the most widely spoken language in Africa, yet Stanford HAI (May 2025) lists it as a textbook low-resource language. It has the speakers, but not the digitized data AI needs to learn it.
- Only about **600 of the world's 7,151 languages are meaningfully supported online**, and **99% of online content sits in just 40 languages** (SIL Global). UNESCO responded in July 2025 by launching an English-Kiswahili AI dictionary, because even basic AI vocabulary barely exists in Kiswahili.

### 1.5 The alternative is now real

- On-device inference costs approach zero per use. Crane AI Labs put it plainly about their offline-first Swahili model. "The ultimate cost-per-inference is close to zero," and it "may be the only economic model that is sustainable for a free consumer app in Africa" (Google DeepMind Gemmaverse).
- The hackathon's own platform, QVAC, runs LLMs, speech, translation, and more entirely on phones and laptops, with no internet, no API keys, and no data leaving the device. Its new **TranslatePsy-AfriSLM** model translates directly between **19 African languages, offline**.

**The meta-problem in one sentence.** The people who would gain most from AI, those with the least connectivity, the tightest budgets, the strongest privacy duties, and non-English mother tongues, are precisely the people cloud AI serves worst.

Composite segments, grounded in the stats above. These are the people a judge in Nairobi will recognize.

| Segment | Their reality | How cloud AI fails them |
|---|---|---|
| Students and teachers | Patchy bundles, English-medium materials, home languages at home | Tools cut out mid-lesson, content ignores their language |
| Market traders and SMEs | Voice-first, Swahili-first, thin margins | Per-token USD pricing is a non-starter, voice data leaves the country |
| Community health workers and field officers | Sensitive client data, rural signal dead zones | Compliance risk, and the app dies exactly where they work |
| Cross-border traders, guides, travelers | Constant language friction, roaming costs | Translation apps need the internet they don't have |
| Local developers and startups | Building for the segments above | USD API bills and latency make their products unsustainable |

## 3. Problem Spaces by Track

The hackathon offers three tracks (details in `docs/1.all-info.md`, section 4). Each maps to a distinct problem space.

### PS-1. Language and information access without internet (Track 1, 700 USDt)

**The problem.** Hundreds of millions of people need to move meaning between English and African languages, or between African languages, in places and moments where connectivity is absent, expensive, or inappropriate to use. A trader negotiating, a parent reading a school letter, a patient understanding instructions, a traveler asking directions.

**Why current options fail.**

- Google Translate and similar apps need internet, spend data, and ship private speech and text to foreign servers.
- Human interpreters are scarce and expensive.
- Phrasebooks and static dictionaries can't hear you, can't answer, and can't adapt.

**Why now.** QVAC's TranslatePsy-AfriSLM (19 African languages, offline) plus on-device transcription and text-to-speech make a fully offline translation experience buildable in a week. Track 1 explicitly suggests live translation as a topic.

### PS-2. Cloud AI bills and data exposure for local builders (Track 2, 900 USDt)

**The problem.** Startups and SMEs already use cloud AI for chatbots, summarization, and support. They pay in dollars, suffer latency, carry compliance risk, and lose the feature entirely when connectivity drops.

**Why now.** Track 2 asks us to migrate a real cloud workload on-device with QVAC and document the quality, speed, memory, and privacy tradeoffs. GSMA's edge-AI research says exactly this migration is the open economic question for the continent.

### PS-3. Self-custody finance with local intelligence (Track 3, 400 USDt)

**The problem.** Custodial finance apps hold users' keys, need connectivity, and offer no private, offline way to understand a transaction before signing it. New self-custody users face irreversible mistakes in a language and interface they barely follow.

**Why now.** WDK gives self-custodial wallets across 50+ chains, and QVAC can explain and check actions locally. Highest complexity of the three tracks, and the smallest dedicated prize.

## 4. What a Good Solution Must Look Like

Any problem we pick must admit a solution with these properties, derived from the judging criteria (real user value, meaningful QVAC integration, working implementation, usability, demo clarity).

1. **Offline-first.** The core feature works in airplane mode, on camera, in one take.
2. **Real hardware.** Runs on a physical phone or laptop we own, since QVAC doesn't support emulators.
3. **Local languages.** Treats African languages as first-class, not as an afterthought.
4. **Free to use.** No per-token bills. Cost per inference close to zero.
5. **Private by default.** Nothing leaves the device for the core feature.
6. **Buildable in seven days.** Small surface, deep QVAC use.

## 5. Candidate Problems and Decision Matrix

Scored 1 to 5 against the judging criteria plus build-week feasibility. Six criteria, 30 points maximum.

| Candidate | User value | QVAC depth | Feasibility | Demo clarity | Differentiation | Prize fit | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Offline voice and text translator, English to Swahili and other AfriSLM pairs** (Track 1) | 5 | 5 | 4 | 5 | 4 | 5 | **28** |
| B. Offline study assistant, RAG over local curriculum notes (Track 1) | 4 | 4 | 4 | 4 | 3 | 5 | 24 |
| C. Cloud-to-pocket migration of an SME chatbot (Track 2) | 4 | 3 | 3 | 4 | 3 | 4 | 21 |
| D. Offline finance explainer with WDK testnet (Track 3) | 3 | 4 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 3 | 19 |

**Reading the matrix.**

- **A** chains the most QVAC capabilities (transcription, translation, LLM, text-to-speech) around the hackathon's own suggested topic, demos beautifully (speak English offline, hear Swahili back), and targets the single largest, best-evidenced user group. Its main risks are model size on mid-range phones and Swahili speech-recognition support, both listed in section 8.
- **B** is the strong fallback. Same track, similar stack, slightly less differentiated (QVAC's own Hiraia showcase is an offline tutor).
- **C** needs a pre-existing cloud workload to migrate, which we don't have. Weak fit for a one-week solo build starting from zero.
- **D** is the boldest but riskiest. Testnet setup plus WDK plus QVAC in seven days, for the smallest track prize.

## 6. Recommended Problem Statement (Draft)

**Chosen candidate.** A, an offline translator for African languages. **Chosen track.** Track 1, Offline AI apps.

Draft text for the DoraHacks "problem description" field, to be refined after `docs/3.research.md`.

> Over 200 million people speak Swahili, and Africa is home to more than 2,000 languages, yet the AI revolution mostly speaks English and lives in the cloud. In Kenya, 52% of the population is offline at any given moment, and pay-as-you-go data costs make every cloud API call a small purchase. The people who need translation most, in markets, clinics, classrooms, and at border crossings, are exactly the people cloud AI serves worst.
>
> Today's translation tools make three demands our users can't meet. They demand internet that isn't there. They demand airtime that isn't free. And they demand your private words, sent to a server in another country.
>
> We are building **[project name TBD]**, a translator that lives entirely on your phone. It hears you, translates between English and Swahili (and other African language pairs), and speaks back, with the internet switched off. No account, no API key, no cloud, no cost per use. Your words never leave your device, because they never have to.

**Why this problem, in judging terms.**

- **Real user value.** Language access is a daily, visceral need for hundreds of millions, and the evidence (sections 1.1, 1.4) quantifies it.
- **Meaningful QVAC integration.** Translation (AfriSLM) is the core, wrapped by on-device transcription and text-to-speech, all QVAC.
- **Working implementation.** Narrow scope, one clear pipeline, physical-device testable.
- **Usability.** One screen, speak or type, get speech or text back.
- **Demo clarity.** Airplane mode, live conversation, unmistakable.

## 8. Open Questions for `docs/3.research.md`

1. Which exact language pairs and model sizes does TranslatePsy-AfriSLM ship, and do English and Swahili both make the list?
2. Does QVAC's on-device transcription (Whisper or Parakeet) handle Swahili speech, and English with Kenyan accents? If not, the v1 scope becomes voice English in, typed Swahili in, voice and text both ways out.
3. End-to-end latency and memory of the translate plus speak pipeline on our target device. Which device do we demo on?
4. Smallest model that still demos well, given judges may run it themselves.
5. Competitor check. What exists on-device for Swahili translation today (Crane AI Labs' Swahili Gemma, Google Translate offline packs), and what do we do better?
